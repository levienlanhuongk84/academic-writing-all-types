2. A 5-step process for any piece of writing

Paragraph, email or essay, good writers do the same 5 things. The first two steps take only a few minutes, but they decide most of your score for task response.

  1. Read and analyse the task. Underline the topic, the audience and the questions you must answer. Ask: Who am I writing to? Why? Which points must I cover?
  2. Brainstorm ideas. Note 2–3 ideas for each requirement, then pick the clearest ideas and the easiest to support. Do not pick a “clever” idea you do not have the words to write about.
  3. Make an outline. Put your ideas into the frame for the task type: one main idea per paragraph, and each main idea with an explanation and an example.
  4. Write. Follow your outline. Write clear sentences first, then longer ones. Start each paragraph with a topic sentence.
  5. Check. Count your words, make sure you have answered every point, then look for your own habitual grammar errors (verb forms, plurals, articles, punctuation).

4. What do examiners mark?

IELTS and VSTEP both mark writing with 4 criteria of equal weight. The names are different, but the content is almost the same, so you can practise for both in the same way.

Criterion 1

Task response

IELTS: Task Achievement (Task 1), Task Response (Task 2) · VSTEP: Task Fulfilment

Does the answer cover every requirement, fully and correctly? Are the ideas developed? Does the tone suit the reader? Does an IELTS Task 1 answer have an overview?

Criterion 2

Coherence and cohesion

IELTS: Coherence & Cohesion · VSTEP: Organization

Are the paragraphs sensible, one idea each, with ideas in a logical order? Are the linking words varied and used correctly, or is it a mechanical “Firstly, Secondly…” pattern?

Criterion 3

Vocabulary

IELTS: Lexical Resource · VSTEP: Vocabulary

Is the vocabulary wide enough, with the right meaning, the right word partners (collocations) and correct spelling? Can the writer paraphrase instead of copying words from the task?

Criterion 4

Grammar

IELTS: Grammatical Range & Accuracy · VSTEP: Grammar

Is there a mix of simple and complex sentences? How many sentences are error-free? Do the errors make the reader misunderstand?

Remember
For IELTS 6.5–7.0 or VSTEP level 4: answer every point, one clear idea per paragraph, give specific examples, and keep most sentences error-free. “Advanced” words used wrongly lose more marks than simple words used correctly.

Correct, effective sentences

A correct sentence needs at least one independent clause: it has a subject and a finite verb, and it expresses one complete idea. This lesson helps you learn the 4 sentence types, join sentences with the right punctuation and avoid the 5 sentence errors that are common with Vietnamese learners.

1. Understand clauses

Independent clause

Has a subject + a finite verb and can stand alone.

2. Four sentence types

Writing at band 6.5+ or VSTEP level 4 needs a mix of sentence types. Only simple sentences sound choppy; only long sentences invite mistakes. An easy balance to control: about half simple or compound sentences, half complex sentences.

3. Three ways to join sentences, and the punctuation for each

Type of connectorCommon wordsPunctuationExample
Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS)for, and, nor, but, or, yet, soa comma before the connectorFees are high, so fewer students apply.
Subordinating conjunctionsbecause, although, while, when, if, unless, since, whereasat the start: a comma after the dependent clause; in the middle: usually no commaBecause fees are high, fewer students apply. / Fewer students apply because fees are high.
Linking adverbshowever, therefore, moreover, as a result, in addition, for examplea semicolon before and a comma after; or start a new sentenceFees are high; therefore, fewer students apply. / Fees are high. As a result, fewer students apply.
Note
However, therefore, moreover cannot join two clauses with a comma. Writing “Fees are high, however many students apply” is a comma splice error.

5. Five common sentence errors and how to fix them

Wrong
Correct
1. Fragment
Because the city is too crowded.A dependent clause standing alone.
Many families move out because the city is too crowded.
2. Run-on
Technology changes fast people must keep learning.Two independent clauses with no connector and no punctuation.
Technology changes fast, so people must keep learning.
3. Comma splice
Exercise is important, it reduces stress.A comma is not strong enough to join two independent clauses.
Exercise is important because it reduces stress. / Exercise is important; it reduces stress.
4. Subject–verb disagreement
The number of tourists have increased.The main subject is the number (singular).
The number of tourists has increased.
5. Dangling modifier
Living in the countryside, the air is fresh.“Living…” must refer to people, but the subject is “the air”.
Living in the countryside, people enjoy fresh air.

Three more “weak sentence” problems

Faulty parallelism

The course is cheap, flexible and it helps you a lot.

→ The course is cheap, flexible and useful. (parts joined by and/or/not only…but also must have the same form)

Choppy sentences

Cars are cheap. Cars are convenient. Cars pollute the air.

→ Although cars are cheap and convenient, they pollute the air.

Stringy, wordy sentences

In my opinion, I think that it is a fact that the reason why… is because…

→ I believe … because … (cut the extra words, one idea per sentence)

Quick practice

  • Combine two facts into one conditional sentence: I don't have a car. I can't drive you. → If I had a car, I could drive you.
  • Turn advice into If I were you, I would… (informal emails).
  • Replace if with unless, provided that, as long as, otherwise, without + N.

Common errors

  • Using will / would in the if clause.
  • Mixing types for no reason (If they invested last year, they would have…).
  • Forgetting the comma when the if clause comes first.
  • In Task 1, use only If current trends continue, … for forecasts.

7. Building sentences from structures (the Dinh Thang method)

  1. Write the idea in Vietnamese and choose a target structure in advance (for example It is + adj + for sb + to V).
  2. Translate it into English using that structure, the way English writers say it, not word for word (for the Vietnamese idea “face difficulties” →have difficulty / struggle to…, not meet difficulty).
  3. Get feedback until the sentence has no basic errors, then copy the correct sentence into your structure notebook.
  4. Rewrite one idea with several sentence openings: It is + adj… / There is a need for… / With + N + V-ing, … / a V-ing subject / the passive.
  5. Reduce two sentences to one: SVO, which… → noun phrase + mean that… (Rising rents mean longer commutes.)
  6. Use them in real essays: reuse 5–10 academic phrases from your notes in each essay, and ask your teacher whether each phrase fits the context.

Writing an academic paragraph

The paragraph is the “building block” of every piece of writing: each IELTS body paragraph and each point in a VSTEP email is a paragraph. A good paragraph deals with one main idea, states it in the first sentence, then proves it with explanation and examples.

120–200 words3 parts5 writing steps
Colour keysubjectverbobject / complementconnectordependent clauserelative clausenoun clausemodifying phrase

1. The 3-part structure of a paragraph

Part 1

Topic sentence

The first sentence of the paragraph. It has a topic + a controlling idea, that is, what you will prove about the topic.

Part 2

Supporting sentences

2–3 main supporting points; each point has minor details: explanation, examples, figures, results. This is the longest part.

Part 3

Concluding sentence

Restate the topic sentence in different words, or sum up the points, or give a final comment.

TS (1 sentence)→Supporting point 1 (1 sentence) + details/examples (2–3 sentences)→Supporting point 2 (1 sentence) + details/examples (2–3 sentences)→CS (1 sentence)

About 8–10 sentences in total. Think of a sandwich: the topic sentence and the concluding sentence are the two slices of bread, and the support is the filling.

Six features of an effective paragraph

1

Clear organisation

All 3 parts, in the right order.

2

Full development

Each supporting point has details and examples.

3

Unity

Only discuss the idea in the topic sentence.

4

Coherence and cohesion

Ideas in logical order, with linking words.

5

Vocabulary

Varied, with synonyms and correct collocations.

6

Sentences

Varied sentence patterns; no fragments, run-ons, comma splices, choppy sentences or rambling sentences.

2. Write the topic sentence

A good topic sentence is not too broad (you cannot prove it in one paragraph), not too narrow (just a fact, with nothing left to write), and not an announcement (“I am going to write about…”).

3. Make an outline: main points and minor details

Before you write, find ideas in one of three ways: listing (note down every idea quickly), a mind map (topic in the middle, branches for supporting points, smaller branches for details), then turn it into a bullet-point outline. Each main point answers the question “why / how?” for the topic sentence; each minor detail makes the main point clearer with an explanation, a description, figures or an example. Ask who, what, when, where, why, how to find details.

Sample outline

TS: Studying abroad helps young people become more independent.

  • Main point 1: manage daily life alone
    • explanation: cook, pay bills, deal with problems without parents
    • example: a student in Australia learns to budget a monthly allowance
  • Main point 2: make decisions about study and career
    • explanation: choose courses, look for part-time jobs
    • result: more confident when they start work

CS: living overseas is a practical lesson in self-reliance.

Tip
The PEEL model is used for IELTS body paragraphs: Point (main point) → Explain (explanation) → Example (example) → Link (link back to the question). It is the same main point and minor detail outline above, written as a short routine.

4. Write connected supporting sentences

Follow your outline, open each main point with a signal word, and use pronouns and repeated key words so the sentences hold together. The full table of linking words is in the lesson Cohesion and paraphrase.

5. Write the concluding sentence

Restate the topic sentence

In short, living overseas teaches young people to stand on their own feet.

Sum up the main points

By managing money and making their own choices, students abroad become far more self-reliant.

Final comment

For this reason, a period of study overseas can be a valuable investment.

Avoid
The concluding sentence does not add a new idea. If you still have a new idea, your outline is not finished.

6. Check unity and coherence

Unity

  • The whole paragraph discusses only one main idea.
  • Every sentence supports the controlling idea. Delete any sentence that goes off topic.

Coherence

  • Repeat key nouns or use synonyms.
  • Keep pronouns consistent (students → they, do not switch to you).
  • Use linking words with the right function.
  • Put ideas in a logical order: time, importance, cause → effect.

9. Reading into writing: summarise an article as a paragraph

This is the core homework task in the Academic Writing course: read an academic article (for example on climate change or hunger), then rewrite its main ideas as a well-structured paragraph. You learn topic vocabulary and practise paragraph structure at the same time.

3. Five paraphrasing techniques

Combine 2–3 techniques in each sentence. Changing the meaning of a sentence is a worse mistake than copying it, so always reread to make sure the meaning stays the same.

  1. Use synonyms. people → individuals; important → essential; show → illustrate
    Only change a word when you are sure it means the same in context. Keep names, figures and technical terms (carbon dioxide) as they are.
  2. Change the word form. The population grew rapidly. → There was rapid growth in the population.
  3. Active ↔ passive. Governments should fund museums. → Museums should be funded by governments.
  4. Change the sentence structure. Many people believe that… → It is widely believed that… / A common belief is that…
  5. Reorder or generalise. in Japan, Korea and China → in three East Asian countries
Formula
Paraphrasing a task sentence = change the words + change the structure. Example: Some people think that university education should be free. → It is sometimes argued that students should not have to pay tuition fees.

From paragraph to essay

An essay is a paragraph “zoomed in”: the topic sentence becomes an introduction with a thesis, each supporting point becomes a body paragraph, and the concluding sentence becomes the conclusion. Once you understand this mapping, an academic essay and an IELTS Task 2 essay follow the same logic.

2. Introduction: Hook – Background – Thesis

An academic introduction works like a funnel: from an attention-grabbing sentence (hook), through a few sentences of context (background), narrowing down to the thesis at the end of the paragraph. 4. Body paragraphs and conclusion

Body paragraphs

  • The topic sentence comes from the thesis (paraphrased, not copied) + the paragraph's controlling idea.
  • Open with a sequence word: The most significant cause… / Another factor… / Finally, …
  • Support with reasons, examples and figures; end with a concluding sentence.

A 3-part conclusion

  1. Restate the thesis with new words and structures.
  2. Sum up each main point in one short sentence, in the same order.
  3. Final comment: a prediction, a recommendation or a call to action.

Do not add new ideas, and avoid I / you in the final comment.

6. How to learn essays: analyse a model → mind map → outline → write

  1. Analyse a model essay: find the hook, the topic, the background, the thesis (main topic + main points), the topic sentence of each body paragraph, and the sentence that restates the thesis in the conclusion.
  2. Check: does the thesis answer the question? Are the main points parallel? Do the body topic sentences paraphrase the thesis?
  3. Draw a mind map: topic → 2–3 main points → 2 supporting points for each → details, examples.
  4. Make an outline: thesis (a full sentence) → topic sentence of each body paragraph + supporting points → conclusion.
  5. Write your essay from the outline, then check it with the checklist in Proofreading and self-assessment.

Sample outline: Causes of urban air pollution

Thesis: three factors: private vehicles, construction dust, waste burning.

  • Body 1 – vehicles: motorbikes and cars rise every year → exhaust fumes at street level → e.g. rush-hour pollution peaks.
  • Body 2 – construction: rapid building of roads and flats → uncovered sites, trucks spread dust → e.g. new metro lines.
  • Body 3 – waste burning: farmers burn straw, households burn rubbish → smoke drifts into the city → worst after harvest.
  • Conclusion: restate + summary + recommendation (cleaner transport, stricter rules on building sites).

VSTEP Writing Task 1 · Lesson 1

Email/letter: task analysis and the 5-part frame

VSTEP Task 1 asks you to write an email or letter replying to a situation. The examiner first checks whether you answer every point you are asked about in a tone that suits the reader. Get these two things right and you have most of the marks.

20 minutesAt least 120 words (aim for 120–150)1/3 of the Writing scoreDo not write your real name or address

1. What the task looks like

Type 1

Email + bullet points

The task gives a short email and 2–3 bullet points that say exactly what you must do. Each bullet point is a required point.

Type 2

Email without bullet points

The task only gives the sender's email (usually from a friend). You have to find every question in the email yourself, including indirect questions such as “I wonder if…”.

4 criteria (each 0–10, equal weight)The examiner asks
Task FulfilmentAre all the points covered? Does each point have details and reasons? Do the tone, greeting and closing suit the reader? Are there at least 120 words?
OrganizationIs there a paragraph for each point? Are the linking words natural?
VocabularyAre words used with the right meaning, the right collocations and the right spelling? Are task sentences copied word for word?
GrammarAre the tenses correct? Are there complex sentences (relative clauses, conditionals)? Do mistakes make it hard to understand?
Score guide
All points covered and well developed: Task Fulfilment 8–10 · all points covered but thin: 7 · one weak point: 6 · one main point missing: 5 · under 120 words: 6 at most. Leaving out a point is the fastest way to lose marks.

2. Analyse the task in 2 minutes: Who – Why – What is asked

Sample task

You received an email from your English friend, Tom:
“Hi! Guess what? I'm coming to Vietnam in December for two weeks! Which city do you think I should visit first? What's the weather like at that time of year? And what food should I try? I can't wait to see you!”
Write a reply to Tom. You should write at least 120 words.

  1. Who is reading? Tom, a close friend → informal tone: Hi Tom, … Best wishes, contractions are fine (I'm, it's).
  2. Why are you writing? To reply to a friend and give advice → an information + advice email. Open by reacting to your friend's good news.
  3. What is asked? Underline and number: (1) visit which city first, (2) weather in December, (3) food to try. Three points = three body paragraphs.

Task 1 asks you to summarise the information in a visual (a chart, table, process or map) in an objective style. The examiner does not need every number; they want to see that you can pick out the main features and compare them.

1. The task and what the examiner expects

Sample task

Must include

  • One opening sentence that introduces the visual (paraphrased).
  • An Overview paragraph with the 2 most striking features.

Must not include

The whole is 100%

IELTS Task 2 · Lesson 1

The essay: analysing the task and making an outline

Task 2 is worth 2/3 of the Writing score. Most essays get low marks not because of grammar but because they answer a different question or do not develop their ideas. The first five minutes, spent analysing the task and outlining, decide the whole essay.

40 minutesAt least 250 words (aim for 260–280)4–5 paragraphsA clear position (when the task asks for your opinion)

1. Analyse the task: three kinds of key words

Sample task

Some people believe that children should be taught how to manage money at school. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

Topic words

Topic words: who, what?

children · manage money

The main subject of the task.

Qualifying words

Limiting words

should be taught · at school

They narrow the issue. Ignore them and you go off topic: the task asks about teaching at school, not at home.

Action words

Instruction words

To what extent do you agree or disagree?

→ Opinion type: state how far you agree and defend it.

Off topic
Writing about “the importance of money” or “education in general” answers the broad topic, not the issue. Every topic sentence in the body must repeat the key words (or synonyms). Missing part of the question (for example, when the task asks both why and positive or negative) usually keeps Task Response at band 5.
Know the difference
A task that states a trend (More people are living alone) is different from one that states an opinion (Some people think…). Do not turn a trend into “Many people think that…”. A task asking agree/disagree is also different from one asking positive/negative or advantages/disadvantages: answer the question you are asked.

3. Brainstorm ideas in 3 minutes

  1. Ask “Why?” about the issue and note 3–4 ideas in simple English (or Vietnamese if you are stuck).
  2. Look at the issue from several angles: individual · family · society · economy · health · environment · education · technology.
  3. Choose the 2 strongest ideas: ones you can explain and give a concrete example for. Drop ideas that “sound good” but that you lack the words to write about.
  4. Attach an example to each idea: a specific situation, a common experience, or a reasonable general figure (do not invent specific studies).

Sample brainstorm for the “teach money management at school” task

  • Individual: avoid debt, instalment buying, “buy now, pay later” → example: a project tracking spending for 1 month.
  • Society / fairness: not every parent can teach this → school is the only place where every child gets to learn it.
  • (Back-up idea) Economy: young people who know how to save and invest → a more stable economy.

6. Body paragraphs with PEEL / PEER

P · Point

A topic sentence with the main idea and the task's key words.

E · Explain

1–2 sentences explaining why / how: This is because… / This can be explained by the fact that…

E · Example

A specific, real-life example: For example… / This is clearly seen in the case of…

L / R · Link / Result

A consequence that leads back to the main idea: As a result… / Consequently…

Two kinds of body paragraph

Single-idea

One idea, developed in depth

Topic sentence → explanation → example → result. Use it for Opinion and Discussion tasks; the safest way to reach band 7+.

Multi-idea

Two ideas in one paragraph

A topic sentence with a plural noun (There are two main reasons why…) → idea 1 + development → idea 2 + development. Use it when the task asks for reasons, problems, solutions, advantages.

BandHow ideas are developed
5–6Ideas are unclear or drift away from the topic sentence; the two body paragraphs are unbalanced (one has 1 idea, the other 3; one is long, the other very short).
7Every idea is extended (consequences, “if… then…”) and supported by an example.
8–9Ideas are fully developed with specific examples; minor points are added briefly with such as, including.
Tip
Developing 2 ideas in depth is better than listing 4 ideas in one sentence each. Examples must be reasonable: say “most students” instead of inventing “75% of students”, and do not use “my uncle” as evidence.

The five Task 2 essay types: frames and thesis statements

Every type uses the 4-paragraph frame. What changes is the thesis statement in the introduction and the content of the two body paragraphs. Learn the frame for each type by heart so you do not have to think about structure in the exam room.

Colour keysubjectverbobject / complementconnectordependent clauserelative clausenoun clausemodifying phrase

1. 1 · Opinion (Agree / Disagree)

Sample task

Some people believe that children should be taught how to manage money at school. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

Option A

Agree (or disagree) completely

  • Introduction: paraphrase + I completely agree that… because…
  • Body 1: reason 1 (PEEL).
  • Body 2: reason 2 (PEEL).
  • Conclusion: For the reasons mentioned above, I maintain that…
Option B

Partly agree

  • Introduction: While I accept that…, I believe that…
  • Body 1: the side you accept (On the one hand, it is true that…).
  • Body 2: the side you support more (Nevertheless, I would argue that…).
  • Conclusion: In conclusion, while…, I still believe…
Note
Your position must be the same in the introduction, body and conclusion. Changing your mind halfway is a serious Task Response error.

2. 2 · Discussion (Discuss both views + your opinion)

Sample task

Some people think that young people should travel or work for a year before starting university. Others believe they should go straight into higher education. Discuss both these views and give your own opinion.

Frame

  • Introduction: paraphrase both views + I believe that…
  • Body 1: the view you do not choose: Some people argue that… This is because…
  • Body 2: the view you choose: However, I believe that… mainly because…
  • Conclusion: summarise both sides + restate your opinion.

Sample thesis statement

While a gap year can broaden young people's horizons, I believe that most students benefit more from starting university immediately.

Present each view “fairly” (explain why people think so), but make your own opinion clear.

For “outweigh” tasks, put the side you think is heavier in body 2 so the essay ends in the direction of your position.

4. 4 · Problem / Cause – Solution

Sample task

Traffic congestion is becoming a serious problem in many cities. What are the causes of this problem, and what measures could be taken to solve it?

Frame

  • Introduction: paraphrase + There are several reasons for this, and a number of measures could be taken to tackle it.
  • Body 1: There are two main reasons why… (2 causes).
  • Body 2: Several measures could be taken to alleviate this problem. (solutions that match each cause).
  • Conclusion: summarise, perhaps add a prediction; no new solutions.

The task uses plurals (causes, solutions) → write at least 2 causes and 2 solutions.

5. 5 · Two-part question (two direct questions)

Sample task

Nowadays more and more people choose to live alone. Why is this happening? Is this a positive or negative development?

Frame

  • Introduction: paraphrase + a short answer to both questions.
  • Body 1: answer question 1 (There are two main reasons why…).
  • Body 2: answer question 2, giving your conclusion in the first sentence (I consider this a largely positive development because…).
  • Conclusion: summarise both answers.

Sample thesis statement

This trend is mainly due to rising incomes and changing attitudes to marriage, and I believe it is a largely positive development.

3. Education

Subtopics: school starting age, homework, university fees, academic or vocational study, life skills, gap year, online learning · Common types: Opinion · Discussion · Adv/Disadv

Common question angles

For free university

5. Society, family and generations

15. What earns and what loses marks in a band 8+ essay?

Earns marks

  • An outline that comes from analysing the task; one job per paragraph.
  • Relevant, developed ideas with specific real-world examples (company names, countries, events).
  • A clear position from start to finish; concede first, then assert (While it is fair to assume…, I would argue…).
  • 10–16 on-topic phrases, used naturally.

Loses marks

  • Many small grammar and vocabulary errors: good ideas, but the overall score stays around 6.
  • Wrong collocations (spend expenses, settle down instead of settle), uncountable nouns with -s (pollutions).
  • Unclear reference words (them, this with no clear meaning).
  • New ideas that do not connect to the previous one; rare words used without control.

Proofreading and self-marking

Three minutes of proofreading at the end can lift your grammar score by half a band. Proofread in three passes. Look for only one kind of problem in each pass, and always check your own habitual mistakes first.

1. Proofread in three passes

  1. Pass 1 · Content. Have you answered every point / every part of the question? Is there an overview (Task 1), a clear position (Task 2), all the points you were asked about (VSTEP)? Have you reached the word count?
  2. Pass 2 · Organisation. Does each paragraph have a topic sentence? One idea per paragraph? Are the linking words correct, and used no more than twice per paragraph? Does the conclusion bring in a new idea?
  3. Pass 3 · Language. Read each sentence and check: subject–verb agreement, tense, plurals, articles, prepositions, punctuation, spelling.

5. Checklist before you submit, by task type

VSTEP Task 1
  • Reread the task and tick each point you have answered.
  • Greeting and sign-off in the same register.
  • One point per paragraph, with a detail + a reason.
  • An opening sentence and a closing sentence.
  • At least 120 words; do not write your real full name.
IELTS Task 1
  • The introduction is paraphrased.
  • The overview has no figures, 2–3 key features.
  • Data chosen by the 4 points: start, end, highest, lowest.
  • Tenses match the time frame; at / to / by / of used correctly.
  • No opinion, no conclusion; 150–200 words.
IELTS Task 2
  • Every part of the question is answered.
  • The same position in the introduction and the conclusion.
  • Each body paragraph: topic sentence → explanation → specific example.
  • The two body paragraphs are of similar length.
  • No new ideas in the conclusion; 260–280 words.
